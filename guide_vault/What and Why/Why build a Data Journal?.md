# Overview

A Data Journal can become an incredibly useful personal system.

Over time it can help you understand life more clearly, build habits of reflection, and capture your life experience in a way whose value compounds over time.

Building your own gives you control to shape it, the **know-how to benefit from it**, and the **buy-in to actually use it**.... plus it teaches you practical technical skills. 

This page explains a few reasons you might consider building one.

## Personal benefits

> [!success] I credit **much** of my health, happiness, and career to my Data Journal

### Better recall of the past

It probably goes without saying, if you write things down, you remember them. 

The faintest pencil is better than the sharpest mind - #todo check that

You can answer questions like:

- When was the last time I saw Derek?
- Where was I three Tuesdays ago? Or this time last year?
- How long ago did my ankle start bothering me?
### Pay more attention (& live better) in the present

Something strange happens when you start tracking habits:  

**You start paying attention to what you're doing.** 

This is a case of the ends provide direction for the means. Not unlike wanting to take sweet pictures for your Instagram so you book a vacation to a beautiful destination - you go in with a very outcome-oriented goal and wind up having a great experience on the journey to pursue it.

Having the level of clarity on your behavior that comes from maintaining a Data Journal also gives you the perspective to see the long-term view of things. I'm tired right now, but I know I want to end this week with more workouts.

You start to think about how you *don't want to write down*:

- "another wasted night"
- "0 workout week"
- "we got takeout again"

### Having a framework for future-focused goals

When you set a New Year's Resolution to "work out more", you're prone to fail. 

When you set a New Year's Resolution to "work out >183 times this year" and then track your progress & extrapolate to the end of the year, it's *a lot easier* to follow through. You've established not only goals, but the system to help you reach them.

## Why build your own

There are apps which promise to help track habits, keep a journal, or quantify aspects of life.

Most of them share the same problem: they are products designed for broad audiences. They want you to work *their* way - and inside their sandbox - for as long as they're around.

People often use these tools enthusiastically for a few weeks or months before abandoning them. Others take to these tools, only to lose them when companies shut down, change business models, or "upgrade" their offerings.

Building your own Data Journal changes that dynamic.

### Ownership

Because the system is yours:

- you control what gets tracked, and how
- you are not dependent on a company's fate or product decisions
- your data remains accessible for decades
- you get the satisfaction of seeing the system you build *work*

This flexibility makes it much easier to maintain the system over long periods of time.

Data Journals are meant to be *yours* — and to work long as *you choose*.

### Skills you develop

Building a Data Journal is a surprisingly great learning project. 

Even a simple version involves skills like:

- working with and organizing data
- writing small automation scripts
- connecting different software tools
- thinking about systems and workflows

These are the same skills used in many modern technical and analytical jobs. 

> [!important] You gradually improve both the system *and* its builder.

### It's fun (& supremely satisfying)

Maybe I'm just weird, but building a bite-sized system in accordance with exactly my own self-determined goals & requirements was (and continues to be) *a lot of fun*. When you learn how to automate some new data pull, the world is your burrito.

Seeing numbers go up is *great*.
